# "Perfect" vs "Great" Films

**Great** is about size — ambition, feeling, cultural weight.
**Perfect** is about inevitability — you can't remove, add, or reorder a scene without
making it worse; the film sets its own rules and never breaks them.

A film can be great but not perfect (Interstellar: the Mann stretch is removable, the
exposition patchable) and perfect without being great (a small thing executed without a
single wrong move).

## The usual candidates, each perfect in a different way

- **12 Angry Men** — the purest case. One room, 96 minutes, twelve characters; every
  line moves the argument or a character. The camera sinks lower as tension rises so
  the ceiling closes in — a formal design executed without a break.
- **Casablanca** — perfect by accident. Studio assembly line, script rewritten during
  shooting, yet no wasted beat; the film screenwriting teachers can't find fat in.
- **Back to the Future** — modern script-perfection. Every first-act joke is
  load-bearing; every setup detonates. People have tried to name a cuttable scene for
  forty years.
- **Seven Samurai / The Godfather** — perfection at scale, the hardest kind: 3+ hours
  with essentially no misjudged scene.
- **Alien / Jaws** — perfect genre machines: pacing, information control, when to show
  and when to withhold.
- **No Country for Old Men** — the recent consensus pick. Almost no score, no
  explanation, never loses you; carved rather than edited.
- **Dr. Strangelove** — tonal perfection, the rarest kind: apocalypse as comedy,
  balanced on a wire for 95 minutes without a wobble.
- **2001: A Space Odyssey** — the edge case that defines the question. Many find it
  glacial, but boredom only counts as a flaw if the pace is a *mistake* — and the
  slowness is the point (scale that dwarfs humanity, and you sit in it). Perfection of
  **intention** (nothing accidental) vs perfection of **pleasure** — accepting that
  distinction decides which side of the Kubrick/Nolan divide you live on.

## The honest answer underneath

No film has zero flaws. "Perfect" means the flaws are **invisible from inside the
film** — everything feels inevitable while you watch. It's an internal-coherence
judgment, not an objective one.

Useful test: a film that feels "stitched together" is one whose seams you can see —
you can imagine scenes moved, cut, done differently (this is exactly the TDK
complaint). A perfect film never lets you imagine an alternative version of itself; it
feels less like something someone made and more like something that could only ever
have been this way.
